## 1. Detecting Cardiovascular Disease from a Routine Check-up

Good morning. My project is patient's sickness prediction. The question is simple: from the values a doctor records at a routine check-up, can a model tell whether a patient has cardiovascular disease? And just as important: how good is it really, compared with what doctors already use?

## 2. Why this matters

*84 words, about 36 seconds*

Cardiovascular diseases are the world's leading cause of death: about 19.8 million people in 2022. Many of those deaths can be delayed if patients at risk are found early. So the objective is a model that uses one examination's values to detect the disease. Three questions guide the evaluation. First, what does machine learning add over what a doctor already has: a blood-pressure threshold and a standard risk score? Second, how much do data-handling choices change the result? Third, can the predictions be trusted?

## 3. The data: 70,000 check-ups

*69 words, about 30 seconds*

The dataset has seventy thousand patients. For each, eleven values: age, sex, height, weight, the two blood-pressure readings, cholesterol and glucose in three levels, and three yes-or-no answers about smoking, alcohol and physical activity. Half the patients have cardiovascular disease. Two warnings from the start: we don't know where the data comes from, and we don't know how disease was defined. I come back to both at the end.

## 4. 2% of the records are impossible

*88 words, about 38 seconds*

The first finding came before any model. About two percent of the records hold values no living person can have, nearly all of them blood pressures: sixteen thousand, minus one hundred and fifty, zero. Most are typing errors with a pattern: an extra zero, centimetres instead of millimetres of mercury, a stray minus sign, or the two readings swapped. And three in four of these records belong to sick patients. Dropped or repaired, the effect is dramatic: systolic pressure's correlation with the diagnosis goes from 0.05 to 0.43.

## 5. What the clean data shows

*70 words, about 30 seconds*

Here is the clean data, on the training patients only. The disease rate climbs steadily with systolic pressure, from about a quarter below 120 to 86 percent at 160 and above. It almost triples with age, rises with weight, and three quarters of patients with very high cholesterol have the disease. The lifestyle answers barely move it, and two point the wrong way: smokers and drinkers have slightly less disease.

## 6. The smoking paradox

*84 words, about 36 seconds*

That is the smoking paradox. Smoking causes heart disease, so I checked for a confounder: most smokers here are men, so I compared men of the same age. The paradox survives: smokers have 3.1 points less disease, and the interval excludes zero. A possible reason, which the data cannot test: diagnosis and answers come from the same visit, so patients already diagnosed may have quit. Either way, this is detection of existing disease, not prediction, and every effect is an association, not a cause.

## 7. What machine learning adds

*98 words, about 42 seconds*

Now the models. Everything was tuned with five-fold cross-validation on the training patients, and these accuracies are on 13,722 test patients the models never saw. At the bottom, the Framingham score as published: 64.6 percent. It was built for a different question, a first event within ten years, and it does not transfer. The guideline rule, systolic at least 130, already gets 71.3. Logistic regression 72.6. Random forest, the neural network and gradient boosting are close together, between 73.1 and 73.6. The best is gradient boosting: 73.6 percent, AUC 0.804. Machine learning helps, but by about two points.

On unseen patients, gradient boosting reaches an AUC of 0.804, with a confidence interval from 0.797 to 0.811, and 73.6 percent accuracy, close to its cross-validated 73.5, so it did not overfit. ## 11. How 90% accuracy can happen

*100 words, about 43 seconds*

Back to the second question. Papers on this dataset report 88 and even 94.5 percent. I reproduced three common mistakes. Choosing the luckiest of 50 random splits barely matters: 73.4. Oversampling with SMOTE before splitting puts near-copies of training patients into the test set: a random forest then reports 86.7 percent, against 71 on truly unseen patients. Scoring a model on its own training patients gives 99.9. Same data, only the evaluation changed. I cannot know what those papers did, but on this data an accuracy far above 74 percent is a reason to check the evaluation before believing it.

## 12. Can the predictions be trusted?

*89 words, about 38 seconds*

Third question: can the risks be trusted? In this sample, yes: patients given 20 percent have the disease about 20 percent of the time. Women and men get similar results. Older patients are harder to separate, 0.72 over 60 against 0.84 under 50, but blood pressure alone drops the same way, so that is the data more than the model. And where one patient in five is sick, only 44 percent of flagged patients would really have it: a positive result means worth a closer look, not a diagnosis.

## 14. Conclusions

*94 words, about 40 seconds*

To conclude. Machine learning adds something real but small: about two points over the guideline rule, and the limit near 74 percent seems to lie in the data. Data handling can move reported numbers far more than the algorithm: dirty records cost the linear model accuracy, and two leakage mistakes report 85 to 100 percent. The predictions are trustworthy within limits. The main limitations: it detects existing disease and cannot forecast, the source and diagnosis definition are unknown, and treatment is not recorded. The next step: data from a known clinic, followed over time.
