## 1. Detecting Cardiovascular Disease from a Routine Check-up

*49 words, about 21 seconds*

Good morning. My project is topic two, patient's sickness prediction. The question is simple: from the values a doctor records at a routine check-up, can a model tell whether a patient has cardiovascular disease? And just as important: how good is it really, compared with what doctors already use?

## 2. Why this matters

*84 words, about 36 seconds*

Cardiovascular diseases are the world's leading cause of death: about 17.9 million people a year. Many of those deaths can be delayed if patients at risk are found early. So the objective is a model that uses one examination's values to detect the disease. Three questions guide the evaluation. First, what does machine learning add over what a doctor already has: a blood-pressure threshold and a standard risk score? Second, how much do data-handling choices change the result? Third, can the predictions be trusted?

## 3. The data: 70,000 check-ups

*69 words, about 30 seconds*

The dataset has seventy thousand patients. For each, eleven values: age, sex, height, weight, the two blood-pressure readings, cholesterol and glucose in three levels, and three yes-or-no answers about smoking, alcohol and physical activity. Half the patients have cardiovascular disease. Two warnings from the start: we don't know where the data comes from, and we don't know how disease was defined. I come back to both at the end.

## 4. 2% of the records are impossible

*94 words, about 40 seconds*

The first finding came before any model. About two percent of the records hold blood pressures no living person can have: sixteen thousand, minus one hundred and fifty, zero. They are not random. Most are typing errors with a pattern: an extra zero, centimetres of mercury instead of millimetres, a stray minus sign, or the two readings swapped. I compared dropping these records with repairing them by rule. Either way the effect is dramatic: in the raw data systolic pressure barely correlates with the diagnosis, 0.05. After cleaning it is the strongest predictor, 0.43.

## 5. What the clean data shows

*70 words, about 30 seconds*

Here is the clean data, on the training patients only. The disease rate climbs steadily with systolic pressure, from about a quarter below 120 to 86 percent at 160 and above. It almost triples with age, rises with weight, and three quarters of patients with very high cholesterol have the disease. The lifestyle answers barely move it, and two point the wrong way: smokers and drinkers have slightly less disease.

## 6. The smoking paradox

*94 words, about 40 seconds*

That is the smoking paradox. Smoking causes heart disease, so I checked for a confounder. Almost only men smoke in this data, so I compared within men, age group by age group. The paradox survives: smokers have less disease at every age. The likely reason is how the data was collected. Diagnosis and answers come from the same visit, and patients who were diagnosed have usually been told to quit. Two consequences: this is detection of existing disease, not prediction of future disease, and every effect we find is an association, not a cause.

## 7. What machine learning adds

*95 words, about 41 seconds*

Now the models. Everything was tuned with five-fold cross-validation on the training patients, and these accuracies are on 13,722 test patients the models never saw. At the bottom, the Framingham score as published: 64.6 percent. It was built for a different question, a first event within ten years, and it does not transfer. The guideline rule, systolic at least 130, already gets 71.3. Logistic regression 72.6. Random forest, the neural network and gradient boosting are tied, around 73.5. The best is gradient boosting: 73.6 percent, AUC 0.804. Machine learning helps, but by about two points.

## 8. On patients the model has never seen

*80 words, about 34 seconds*

On unseen patients, gradient boosting reaches an AUC of 0.804, with a confidence interval from 0.797 to 0.811, and 73.6 percent accuracy, the same as in cross-validation, so it did not overfit. The most concrete way to see the gain: at exactly the rule's specificity, the model finds 301 more of the 6,785 diseased patients. And the learning curve is flat, so more data of the same kind would not help: the ceiling is set by what the examination records.

## 9. What drives the prediction

*73 words, about 31 seconds*

What does the model rely on? Scramble the blood-pressure features and the AUC drops by 0.21. Age and cholesterol follow, at 0.036 and 0.030. Glucose, the lifestyle answers and sex add almost nothing. The shape matters too: predicted risk stays around a third below 125, jumps to 84 percent by 150, and then stays flat. It works like a switch at the guideline's thresholds, which is exactly what a straight-line model cannot capture.

## 10. A score a doctor can add up

*90 words, about 39 seconds*

Since the gains are small, I also built something a doctor can use with a pen. Four factors, and each band earns whole points: age, systolic pressure, BMI and cholesterol. I left out the lifestyle answers, because in this data they would earn negative points, and no one can hand out a score that rewards smoking. Add up the points and the total maps to a risk. This card reaches an AUC of 0.793, the same as logistic regression on all fifteen features, and only 0.011 below the best model.

## 11. How 90% accuracy happens

*90 words, about 39 seconds*

Back to the second question. Papers on this same dataset report 88 and even 94.5 percent. I reproduced three common mistakes. Choosing the luckiest of 50 random splits barely matters: 73.4. Oversampling with SMOTE before splitting puts near-copies of training patients into the test set: 86.7 percent. Scoring a model on its own training patients gives 99.9. Same data, same models, only the evaluation changed. I cannot know what those papers did, but on this data an accuracy far above 74 percent should be read as a sign of leakage.

## 12. Can the predictions be trusted?

*92 words, about 39 seconds*

Third question: can the risks be trusted? The model is well calibrated: patients given 20 percent have the disease about 20 percent of the time. Women and men are served equally. It is weaker for patients over 60, an AUC of 0.72 against 0.84 under 50, because most older patients have high pressure and most have the disease. And in a check-up population where one patient in five is sick, only 44 percent of flagged patients would really have it. So a positive result means: worth a closer look, not a diagnosis.

The deliverable is a working tool. The Python file trains and saves the model, then checks a new patient from the command line. It rejects impossible readings, with the same rules as the cleaning, and returns the risk, the verdict at two cut-offs, one for accuracy and one for screening, and the clinical points with their breakdown. ## 14. Conclusions

*88 words, about 38 seconds*

To conclude. Machine learning adds something real but small: two points over the guideline rule, and the ceiling of about 74 percent is set by the data. Data handling matters more than the algorithm: cleaning rescues linear models, and leakage can manufacture almost any accuracy. And the predictions are trustworthy within limits. The main limitations: this detects existing disease and cannot forecast, the data's source and diagnosis definition are unknown, and treatment is not recorded. The next step would be data from a known clinic, followed over time.
